<WhyItMatters>
You have been finding `kS`, `kV`, and `kG` by nudging numbers and watching graphs. That works, but it is slow, and a new robot means starting again.
**System identification** (SysId) measures the constants instead: run the mechanism through a few fixed tests and let a tool fit the numbers.
</WhyItMatters>

## The four tests

SysId drives the mechanism with a known voltage and records the voltage, position, and velocity. It runs two kinds of test, each in both directions.

| Test | What the voltage does | What it reveals |
|---|---|---|
| Quasistatic | Rises slowly, for example 1 V per second | Friction and back-EMF at each speed: `kS` and `kV` |
| Dynamic | Jumps to a fixed step, for example 7 V | How quickly the mechanism speeds up: `kA` |

Run each in forward and reverse, which makes four routines. Going both ways separates gravity (`kG`) from friction, because gravity helps one direction and fights the other.

A slow ramp keeps acceleration near zero, so what is left is friction plus speed. A sudden step makes acceleration large, so the extra voltage it needs tells you `kA`.

## What your code provides

WPILib's `SysIdRoutine` class does the sequencing. You give it three things:

1. **A way to apply a voltage** to the motors, such as a method that calls `setVoltage`.
2. **A way to log**: each loop, record the voltage, position, and velocity for that mechanism.
3. **The subsystem**, so the test commands require it and nothing else can drive the mechanism meanwhile.

You then bind the routine's four commands (a quasistatic and a dynamic one for each direction) to buttons. The default ramp rate, step voltage, and timeout can be changed in its config.

The result is a log file. Open it in the SysId tool, which ships with WPILib, choose the mechanism type (elevator, arm, or simple motor), and it reports the fitted constants.

## Safety and trust

- The mechanism needs room to move. A ramp keeps speeding up until the test ends, so an elevator can reach its top or bottom stop.
- Keep a hand on the disable key, and start with the mechanism in the middle of its travel.
- Soft limits and current limits stay on.
- The fitted constants are a **starting point**. Put them in, then check the graphs as you did in lesson 3.5. Bad data, such as a mechanism that hit a stop, produces bad constants.

<GoDeeper title="why the units must match">
The tool fits constants in the units you logged. If position is in meters, `kV` comes out in volts per meter per second. Log a different unit than your feedforward code uses and the numbers will be wrong by a constant factor.
</GoDeeper>

## Checkpoint

<Checkpoint lesson="3.11">

<details>
<summary>Why run each test in both directions?</summary>
Friction is the same both ways but gravity is not. Comparing the two directions lets the tool separate them, which gives `kG` for an elevator or arm.
</details>

<details>
<summary>You get constants from SysId. What do you do next?</summary>
Enter them in the feedforward and graph the mechanism following a target. Adjust if the graph shows steady error or overshoot. The constants are a starting point, not a final answer.
</details>

</Checkpoint>

## Recap

<RecapCard>
- SysId measures feedforward constants from logged voltage, position, and velocity.
- Quasistatic is a slow ramp (`kS`, `kV`). Dynamic is a step (`kA`). Both run forward and reverse.
- `SysIdRoutine` needs a voltage method, a logging method, and the subsystem.
- Leave room to move, be ready to disable, and verify the result on graphs.
</RecapCard>
