<WhyItMatters>
Seeing a tag tells you where the tag is relative to the camera. A shooter or an auto path needs to know where the robot is on the field. Getting from one to the other takes a chain of facts, and one wrong link makes the pose wrong by a constant amount or makes it jump.
</WhyItMatters>

## The chain of known things

The robot's field position is worked out from three pieces:

1. **Field layout** (known): where each tag sits on the field. WPILib ships it as an `AprilTagFieldLayout` for each season.
2. **Camera-to-tag** (measured by vision): where the tag is relative to the camera.
3. **Robot-to-camera** (measured by you): where the camera is mounted on the robot and which way it faces. This is a `Transform3d`, a 3D offset plus a rotation.

Work backwards: the tag's field position minus the camera-to-tag gives the camera's field position, and removing the robot-to-camera offset gives the robot's. `PhotonPoseEstimator` does this for you. You supply the layout and the robot-to-camera transform.

**Robot-to-camera is yours to get right.** The team's constants measure it from the robot's center, in meters and radians.

## One tag or many

A single flat tag can look the same from two different positions, so one tag has **pose ambiguity**. The estimate may be the mirror-image answer, and the robot pose jumps.

With two or more tags in view, only one robot position fits all of them. Multi-tag results are much more reliable, and PhotonVision can solve them on the coprocessor.

The team's code tries multi-tag first and falls back to the single result with the lowest ambiguity.

## How much to trust it

A pose estimator blends odometry with each vision measurement. A **standard deviation** says how uncertain a measurement is: small means "trust this a lot", large means "barely listen". The team's `OrbitCamera` picks the numbers for every estimate.

<AnnotatedCode
  file="rebuilt2026-7.2/std-devs"
  title="OrbitCamera.java"
  notes={[
    { lines: '1-2', text: 'Several tags: switch to the smaller multi-tag values, so the measurement counts for more.' },
    { lines: '4-5', text: 'One tag more than 4 m away, or any tag with ambiguity above 0.2: set the uncertainty to the largest possible number, which makes the drivetrain ignore it.' },
    { lines: '6-8', text: 'Otherwise grow the uncertainty with distance squared. Farther tags are noisier, so they count for less.' },
  ]}
/>

The last step feeds the estimate to the drivetrain **with its timestamp**, so the pose estimator can apply it to where the robot was when the picture was taken (lesson 7.1).

<GoDeeper title="why the timestamp matters">
Suppose the robot moves 2 m/s and the picture is 40 ms old. The robot has moved 8 cm since. If you applied the measurement as "now", every correction would pull the pose 8 cm behind. The pose estimator keeps a short history of odometry and applies the measurement to the matching moment.
</GoDeeper>

## Checkpoint

<Checkpoint lesson="7.2">

<details>
<summary>Name the three known things that give the robot's field position from one tag.</summary>
The field layout (where the tag is), camera-to-tag (measured by vision), and robot-to-camera (measured by you and stored as a constant).
</details>

<details>
<summary>Why does the team's code give a far single-tag result huge uncertainty?</summary>
Distance makes the estimate noisy, and a single tag may be the wrong mirror solution. A huge standard deviation tells the drivetrain to ignore it, so it does not make the pose jump.
</details>

</Checkpoint>

## Recap

<RecapCard>
- Robot pose = tag field position, camera-to-tag (vision), and robot-to-camera (your constant).
- Multi-tag estimates remove the ambiguity that single tags have.
- Standard deviations decide how much each measurement is trusted; ambiguity and distance make them larger.
- The estimate goes to `addVisionMeasurement` with its capture timestamp.
- Jumps, constant offsets, and silence each point to a different link in the chain.
</RecapCard>
