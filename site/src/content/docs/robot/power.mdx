<WhyItMatters>
Your robot is pushing another robot when it suddenly stutters, and the driver loses control for a moment. Nothing is broken.
The robot ran short of electrical power and shut its motors off to protect itself. Knowing how power flows tells you how to prevent it.
</WhyItMatters>

## The power path

Everything on the robot is powered by one battery. Power flows in one direction through three devices:
battery, then the main breaker, then the power distribution hub, which gives each device its own protected channel.

The order matters. The breaker protects the whole robot. Each channel protects one wire, so one shorted device does not take down the rest.

## Voltage sags under load

A battery is not a perfect 12 V source. It has **internal resistance**, a small resistance inside the battery itself. Current flowing through it wastes some voltage, so the voltage you actually get drops as current rises:

`battery voltage ≈ resting voltage − current × internal resistance`

The internal resistance is about 0.02 Ω (ohms). At 200 A, that costs about 4 V, so a 12.7 V battery delivers only about 8.7 V. This drop is called **voltage sag**.

If the voltage falls too far, the roboRIO has a safety rule. A **brownout** happens when the roboRIO sees less than 6.75 V (6.3 V on the original roboRIO). It then disables motor outputs to protect itself.
With the motors off, the current falls, the voltage recovers, and the motors come back. If the demand is still there, it repeats, and the robot stutters.

## Two kinds of protection

Brownouts come from asking for too much current. Two layers of protection limit that, and they guard different things.

- **Software current limits** are set in your code. They protect the motor from heat and keep the battery voltage up.
- **Breakers and fuses** are hardware. They protect the wiring, and they only act after a wire is already carrying too much.

A Kraken X60 motor can draw over 300 A if it stalls with no limit. Phoenix 6 has two limits. The **stator limit** caps the current inside the motor, which sets its force. The **supply limit** caps the current drawn from the battery side, which controls total robot power draw. Setting a value does nothing until you also enable it:

```java
new CurrentLimitsConfigs()
    .withSupplyCurrentLimit(FlywheelConstants.FLYWHEEL_SUPPLY_CURRENT_LIMIT_AMPS)
    .withSupplyCurrentLimitEnable(true);
```

The numbers in the lab are examples. A real mechanism needs limits chosen for its motor, gearbox and load.

## Checkpoint

<Checkpoint lesson="1.3">

<details>
<summary>Why does a robot's battery voltage drop when many motors run hard?</summary>
The battery has internal resistance. More current means more voltage lost inside the battery, so less reaches the robot.
</details>

<details>
<summary>What is the difference between a software current limit and a PDH breaker?</summary>
A software limit protects the motor and keeps battery voltage up. A breaker or fuse protects the wiring from overheating.
</details>
