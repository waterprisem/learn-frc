<WhyItMatters>
An arm holds still at 0 degrees and swings wildly at 60 degrees, because code that wanted radians was handed degrees. It compiles, it runs, and the numbers are just wrong.
This lesson covers the small amount of math that robot code uses, and the later lesson that needs each piece.
</WhyItMatters>

## Units come first

A bare number like `40` means nothing to a robot. Forty what? Rotations per second, degrees per second, and meters per second all look the same in code, and mixing them up is the most common bug in robot programming.

That is why the labs put units in names: `targetRPS`, `heightMeters`, `MAX_VELOCITY_DEGREES_PER_SECOND`.

To change units, multiply by a fraction that equals 1. The unit you want to remove goes on the bottom so it cancels.

| Start | Multiply by | Result |
|---|---|---|
| 40 rotations per second | 60 seconds / 1 minute | 2400 rotations per minute |
| 0.25 rotations | 360 degrees / 1 rotation | 90 degrees |
| 90 degrees | 1 rotation / 360 degrees | 0.25 rotations |

If the units do not cancel to what you wanted, the arithmetic is wrong. Lesson 1.8 does this for gear ratios.

## Radians

A full turn is 360 degrees, or 1 rotation, or 2π radians (about 6.28). One radian is the angle where the arc along the edge is as long as the radius. Mathematicians prefer radians because the formulas get simpler.

Same fraction trick: degrees × π / 180 gives radians.

| Degrees | Rotations | Radians |
|---|---|---|
| 360 | 1 | 6.28 (2π) |
| 180 | 0.5 | 3.14 (π) |
| 90 | 0.25 | 1.57 (π / 2) |
| 57.3 | 0.16 | 1 |

WPILib's trig and `ArmFeedforward` expect radians. Lab 7's arm keeps its angles in degrees, so the code converts at the last moment:

```java
armFF.calculate(Math.toRadians(setpoint.position), Math.toRadians(setpoint.velocity));
```

`Units.degreesToRadians` does the same thing. Skipping the conversion is the "radians trap" you will meet in lesson 3.8.

## Sine and cosine

Picture a stick pointing at an angle. Cosine says how much of it is horizontal, and sine says how much is vertical, each as a fraction of the stick's full length.

For an arm, the angle is measured up from horizontal. Gravity pulls hardest on a horizontal arm and not at all on a straight-up one, so the holding effort follows the cosine of the angle:

| Arm angle | cos(angle) | Gravity effort vs. flat |
|---|---|---|
| 0 degrees | 1 | full |
| 60 degrees | 0.5 | half |
| 90 degrees | 0 | none |

Calculators and `Math.cos` need radians, so convert first. Lesson 3.8 uses this exact table.

## Vectors

A **vector** is a quantity with a direction. You can write it two ways: as a speed and a direction, or as an x part and a y part.

| Speed and direction | x part (forward) | y part (left) |
|---|---|---|
| 2 m/s straight ahead | 2 | 0 |
| 2 m/s, 90 degrees left | 0 | 2 |
| 2 m/s, 45 degrees left | 1.41 | 1.41 |

Cosine gives the x part and sine gives the y part. A swerve robot needs exactly this to turn a joystick push into a speed and angle for each wheel (lesson 6.2).

## Position, velocity, acceleration

These are three views of one motion. **Velocity** is how fast position changes. **Acceleration** is how fast velocity changes.

On a graph of position over time, velocity is the slope: steep means fast, flat means stopped. On a velocity graph, acceleration is the slope.

| Elevator moment | Position graph | Velocity | Acceleration |
|---|---|---|---|
| Speeding up | curving steeper | rising | positive |
| Cruising | straight slanted line | constant | zero |
| Slowing to a stop | flattening out | falling to 0 | negative |

A motion profile (lesson 3.6) is a plan for exactly these three moments, so the elevator starts and stops smoothly.

<GoDeeper title="why units libraries exist">
WPILib has a units library with types such as `Distance` and `AngularVelocity`, so the compiler can refuse to add meters to degrees.
The labs use plain `double` values with units in the names instead, which is simpler to start with. The naming habit gives you most of the protection.
</GoDeeper>

<details>
<summary>A mechanism moves 3 rotations in 2 seconds. How do you find its speed in degrees per second?</summary>

Speed is 1.5 rotations per second. Multiply by 360 degrees per rotation to get 540 degrees per second.

</details>

<details>
<summary>On a position graph, what does a flat section mean, and what does a steepening curve mean?</summary>

Flat means velocity is zero, so the mechanism is stopped. A steepening curve means velocity is increasing, so the mechanism is accelerating.

</details>

<RecapCard>
- Every number needs a unit, and the unit goes in the name.
- Convert units by multiplying by a fraction equal to 1 so the old unit cancels.
- Degrees times π over 180 gives radians; WPILib trig and `ArmFeedforward` want radians.
- Cosine is the horizontal part and sine the vertical part; arm gravity follows the cosine of the angle from horizontal.
- Velocity is the slope of position, and acceleration is the slope of velocity.
