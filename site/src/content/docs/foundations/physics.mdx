<WhyItMatters>
Tuning constants such as `kS`, `kG`, `kV`, and `kA` look like magic numbers until you know what each one pays for.
Each is the voltage that cancels one physical effect. Knowing the effect tells you which constant to change when the robot misbehaves.
</WhyItMatters>

## Voltage and current

Think of water in a pipe. **Voltage** is the pressure, the push. **Current** is the flow, measured in amps (A).

Your code commands voltage. A motor controller applies it, and a fresh battery gives about 12 V. How much current flows then depends on what the motor is doing: a motor held still draws a lot, a spinning one draws less.

Current is what makes **torque** (turning force) and heat. Lesson 1.3 covers current limits and battery sag, so this lesson stays on what voltage does.

## Voltage sets speed, current sets torque

For a motor, two rules of thumb carry most of the weight:

- Speed follows voltage. Double the voltage and the free-spinning speed roughly doubles.
- Torque follows current. Double the current and the turning force roughly doubles.

That is why `kV` is **volts per unit of speed**. If a flywheel needs about 5 V to hold 40 rotations per second, then each rotation per second costs about 0.12 V.

## Friction

Rub your hand across a table and it resists before it ever moves. Gears, bearings, and belts do the same. A very small voltage produces no motion because it is only fighting friction.

`kS` is that threshold, a small constant voltage added so the mechanism actually starts moving. Too little and small commands do nothing. Too much and it jumps.

## Gravity

A raised elevator carriage wants to fall. Even standing still, the motor must push up with a constant effort, so you add a constant voltage: `kG`. In the Lab 6 elevator it is 0.55 V.

An arm is different, because gravity pulls less as the arm rises. Its holding voltage is `kG × cos(angle)`, using the cosine from lesson 0.8. In Lab 7, `kG` is 0.16 V: full at flat, half at 60 degrees, zero straight up.

## Inertia

Heavy things resist changes in speed. That is **inertia**. Pushing a loaded shopping cart takes more effort to start than to keep rolling.

Holding a steady speed needs `kV`. Changing speed needs extra voltage on top, and that is `kA`, volts per unit of acceleration. A flywheel has lots of inertia, so it takes noticeable time to spin up, even at full voltage.

## Torque versus speed

Gearing trades one for the other. A reduction gives the mechanism more torque and less speed, and that is why arms use big ratios (lesson 1.8).

## The summary table

| Physical effect | What you see on the robot | Constant | Lesson |
|---|---|---|---|
| Friction | Small commands do nothing, then it jumps | `kS` | 3.4 |
| Gravity (elevator) | Carriage sags with no command | `kG` | 3.7 |
| Gravity (arm) | Holds at some angles, droops at others | `kG` times cosine | 3.8 |
| Speed needs voltage | Steady speed settles too low or too high | `kV` | 3.5 |
| Inertia | Lags behind during speed changes | `kA` | 3.10 |

<GoDeeper title="why feedback still matters">
These constants are a prediction made from a model of the mechanism. A battery sags, a game piece adds weight, and friction changes as parts wear.
PID feedback measures what really happened and corrects the leftover error, which is why robots use both.
</GoDeeper>

## Checkpoint

<Checkpoint lesson="0.9">

<details>
<summary>An elevator drifts down when you command 0 V, but an arm held straight up does not. What explains the difference?</summary>

The elevator fights gravity at every height, so it needs a constant `kG`. The arm's gravity effort follows the cosine of its angle, and cosine of 90 degrees is 0.

</details>

<details>
<summary>Why can a flywheel hold 40 rotations per second with a small voltage but still take a while to reach it?</summary>

Holding speed only has to beat friction and drag, which `kS` and `kV` cover. Reaching speed means changing it, and inertia resists that change. That resistance is what `kA` pays for.

</details>

</Checkpoint>
